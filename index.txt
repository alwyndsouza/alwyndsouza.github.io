<p>Using AI-powered problem framing and sprint-goal generation to align agile teams around outcomes instead of task lists.</p>
</blockquote>
<p><strong>Agent skill GitHub Repo link in references.</strong></p>
<p>Most sprint failures are not caused by poor execution.</p>
<p>They happen because teams begin implementation before alignment exists.</p>
<p>A vague requirement enters backlog refinement, gets converted into stories too quickly, and turns into sprint commitments that optimise delivery activity instead of business outcomes. The result is predictable: teams complete tickets but still miss the actual objective.</p>
<p>This is where the <strong>sprint-goal-writer</strong> and <strong>problem-framing</strong> Agent Skills become valuable.</p>
<p>Rather than treating sprint planning as a story aggregation exercise, these skills introduce structured problem definition and outcome-driven sprint goals directly into the developer workflow.</p>
<p>The goal is simple:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Define the right problem before delivery starts, then ensure the sprint has a measurable outcome once work is committed.</em></p>
</blockquote>
<h3>Why Most Sprint Goals Fail</h3>
<p>Many sprint goals are simply renamed task lists.</p>
<p>Examples like:</p>
<p><em>“Complete payment integration and finish notification service.”</em></p>
<p>do not provide:</p>
<ul>
<li>direction,</li>
<li>prioritisation guidance,</li>
<li>or meaningful trade-off leverage during the sprint.</li>
</ul>
<p>When blockers appear or scope changes, these goals collapse because they describe <em>activities</em>, not <em>outcomes</em>.</p>
<p>A strong sprint goal should answer:</p>
<p><em>“What meaningful change will exist for the customer or business at the end of this sprint?”</em></p>
<p>That distinction changes how teams plan, prioritise, and make delivery decisions.</p>
<h3>The sprint-goal-writer Agent Skill</h3>
<p>The <strong>sprint-goal-writer</strong> skill converts committed sprint stories into a concise, outcome-oriented sprint objective aligned with Scrum principles.</p>
<p>Instead of summarising tickets, it synthesises:</p>
<ul>
<li>business intent,</li>
<li>user impact,</li>
<li>and delivery scope</li>
</ul>
<p>into a single operational goal.</p>
<p>Example transformation:</p>
<h4>Weak Sprint Goal</h4>
<p><em>Complete authentication refactor and implement notifications.</em></p>
<h4>Strong Sprint Goal</h4>
<p><em>Enable customers to receive real-time transaction updates after completing purchases, reducing support dependency through improved visibility.</em></p>
<p>The second version provides the following:</p>
<ul>
<li>a user outcome,</li>
<li>a measurable direction,</li>
<li>and a decision-making framework for the sprint team.</li>
</ul>
<h3>The Five-Point Sprint Goal Validation Model</h3>
<p>Every generated sprint goal is evaluated against five validation checks.</p>
<h4>1. Single Sentence</h4>
<p>The goal should be concise enough to communicate clearly during standups and sprint reviews.</p>
<h4>2. Outcome-Oriented</h4>
<p>It must describe a result, not implementation tasks.</p>
<h4>3. Beneficiary-Focused</h4>
<p>The goal should clearly identify who benefits from the outcome.</p>
<h4>4. Measurable or Directional</h4>
<p>Teams should be able to determine whether the goal was achieved.</p>
<h4>5. Trade-Off Enabling</h4>
<p>The goal should help teams decide what matters most when priorities shift mid-sprint.</p>
<p>This transforms sprint goals from planning artefacts into operational alignment tools.</p>
<p><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/938/1*cT-cTfZYjKtP9kkugn9zzg.png" alt=""></p>
<h3>Problem Framing Before Sprint Planning</h3>
<p>The sprint goal skill works best when paired with the <strong>problem-framing</strong> Agent Skill.</p>
<p>This skill implements the MITRE Problem Framing Canvas — a structured framework used to refine vague requests into actionable problem definitions before stories are written.</p>
<p><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/840/1*BbSO6fer3YFOydp8Co0mDQ.png" alt=""></p>
<p>The framework moves through three stages:</p>
<h4>Phase 1 — Look Inward</h4>
<ul>
<li>Current state</li>
<li>Desired state</li>
<li>Gap analysis</li>
</ul>
<h4>Phase 2 — Look Outward</h4>
<ul>
<li>Stakeholders</li>
<li>Constraints</li>
<li>Risks and assumptions</li>
</ul>
<h4>Phase 3 — Reframe</h4>
<ul>
<li>Refined problem statement</li>
<li>“How Might We” (HMW) question</li>
</ul>
<p>The HMW question becomes the bridge between business intent and implementation planning.</p>
<p>Instead of asking:</p>
<p><em>“How do we build feature X?”</em></p>
<p>teams begin asking:</p>
<p><em>“How might we improve customer onboarding while reducing support dependency and maintaining compliance constraints?”</em></p>
<p>That shift dramatically improves backlog quality before engineering effort begins.</p>
<h3>The End-to-End Workflow</h3>
<p>The complete workflow becomes:</p>
<h4>1. Problem Framing</h4>
<p>Convert vague business requests into refined problem statements and HMW questions.</p>
<h4>2. Story Generation</h4>
<p>Generate structured agile stories aligned with the framed problem.</p>
<h4>3. Story Splitting</h4>
<p>Automatically decompose oversized stories into vertically sliced deliverables.</p>
<h4>4. Sprint Goal Definition</h4>
<p>Generate a measurable sprint outcome aligned with customer or business impact.</p>
<p>This creates a delivery pipeline focused on:</p>
<ul>
<li>alignment,</li>
<li>clarity,</li>
<li>and measurable outcomes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Not just ticket throughput.</p>
<h3>Practical Use Cases</h3>
<h4>During Sprint Planning</h4>
<p>Paste committed stories into /sprint-goal-writer to generate an outcome-focused sprint goal.</p>
<h4>During Retrospectives</h4>
<p>Run historical sprint goals through the validation model to identify weak planning patterns.</p>
<h4>During Discovery</h4>
<p>Use /problem-framing before backlog refinement to reduce ambiguity and improve stakeholder alignment.</p>
<h4>During Scope Trade-Offs</h4>
<p>Use the sprint goal as the prioritisation anchor when blockers or scope changes occur.</p>
<h3>Why This Matters</h3>
<p>High-performing agile teams are not defined by velocity alone.</p>
<p>They are defined by:</p>
<ul>
<li>clarity of intent,</li>
<li>shared understanding,</li>
<li>and disciplined decision-making.</li>
</ul>
<p>Agent Skills like problem-framing and sprint-goal-writer do more than automate documentation.</p>
<p>They encode delivery discipline directly into the engineering workflow.</p>
<p>The result is:</p>
<ul>
<li>better sprint alignment,</li>
<li>fewer misinterpreted requirements,</li>
<li>stronger backlog quality,</li>
<li>and more outcome-driven delivery cycles.</li>
</ul>

<h3>The Hidden Dysfunction at the Heart of Most Agile Teams</h3>
<p>Agile transformations have been underway in software organisations for two decades. Scrum certifications are abundant. Story point poker is practically a professional sport. And yet, in refinement sessions the world over, teams are still doing something that silently undermines every agile principle they claim to follow: splitting stories horizontally.</p>
<p>It’s the default instinct. A story lands in the backlog that’s clearly too big — 13 points, six acceptance criteria, and a scope that spans the UI, the API, and the database. Someone in the room says the words: <em>“Let’s just split it into backend and frontend.”</em> Heads nod. The ticket gets cloned. Two new stories appear, and nobody notices that they’ve just recreated a miniature waterfall inside a sprint that was supposed to be self-contained.</p>
<p>This isn’t a competence problem. Most engineers and product managers understand agile in theory. The issue is that pattern recognition under time pressure defaults to technical decomposition — the mental model we use when we think about architecture — rather than value decomposition, which is the mental model agile actually requires.</p>
<p>I built the agile-story-splitter Copilot Agent Skill to address exactly this gap. But the more I&#39;ve worked with it, the more I&#39;ve come to see it as a symptom of a larger insight: the way we split stories reveals how we think about software delivery, and most of us have been thinking about it wrong.</p>
<h3>Two Mental Models of Software Delivery</h3>
<p>There are two fundamentally different ways to think about how software gets built and delivered.</p>
<p>The first is the <strong>layer model</strong>. In this view, a system is a stack — database at the bottom, business logic in the middle, interface at the top. Work flows downward: you build the foundation before you build the structure. This model is deeply intuitive for engineers because it mirrors how we think about architecture. It also maps neatly to organisational structures – backend teams, frontend teams, QA teams – which makes it feel like the natural unit of decomposition.</p>
<p>The second is the <strong>value model</strong>. In this view, a system is a collection of user-facing capabilities. Work is organised around capabilities, not layers. Each unit of delivery is a thin vertical slice that spans all layers and produces something a user can actually experience and react to.</p>
<p>The tension between these two models is the root cause of bad story splitting. When we default to the layer model — consciously or not — we produce horizontal cuts: stories that represent partial implementations of a capability across a single technical layer. When we apply the value model, we produce vertical slices: stories that represent complete, albeit narrow, implementations of a user-facing capability across all layers.</p>
<p>Imagine a five-layer cake. Cutting it horizontally gives you a plate of sponge, a plate of jam, a plate of cream — technically all the components of the cake, but none of them is a cake you can eat. Cutting it vertically gives you five smaller slices, each containing sponge, jam, and cream in proportion. Each is a complete, edible piece of cake. This is the difference between horizontal and vertical story splitting. Horizontal cuts produce components. Vertical cuts produce value.</p>
<p><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*g3xgo_oBmSG4-MFfo0ziAg.png" alt=""></p>
<p>The INVEST framework — Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable — is the formal articulation of what a vertical slice looks like. A story that fails the “Independent” or “Valuable” criteria almost always does so because it’s a horizontal cut: a component that can’t be shipped or demonstrated without its counterpart layers. The framework is widely known. The failure mode it’s designed to prevent is still everywhere.</p>
<h3>Why This Matters More Now Than It Ever Has</h3>
<p>The argument for vertical splitting has always been theoretically sound. But I’d argue it’s become strategically urgent in the current moment for three reasons.</p>
<p><strong>First, AI-assisted development is compressing implementation time.</strong> When it took a team a week to build an API endpoint, horizontal splitting was inefficient but survivable. When a capable engineer with Copilot can produce a working endpoint in hours, the coordination overhead of horizontal dependencies becomes proportionally more expensive. The bottleneck is no longer writing code — it’s integrating, validating, and shipping it. Stories that create dependencies between layers now consume more sprint capacity in coordination than they do in implementation.</p>
<p><strong>Second, stakeholder feedback loops are shortening.</strong> Product teams are being asked to validate assumptions faster, to run tighter experiment cycles, and to get working software in front of users earlier. A story that can’t be demoed at the end of a sprint — because it’s waiting on its dependent layer — breaks the feedback loop entirely. Vertical slices are not just a delivery best practice; they’re a prerequisite for the kind of rapid validation that modern product development demands.</p>
<p><strong>Third, AI tools are increasingly capable of enforcing standards at the point of work.</strong> The pattern knowledge that used to live in the heads of a few senior engineers — or in a Confluence page nobody reads — can now be embedded directly into the workflow as a skill that activates exactly when it’s needed. This is why building agile-story-splitter as a Copilot Agent Skill matters. It&#39;s not about automating story splitting; it&#39;s about making the right mental model available at the moment the wrong one would otherwise take over.</p>
<h3>The Eight Split Patterns: A Decision Framework</h3>
<p>The agile-story-splitter skill encodes all eight patterns from Lawrence and Green&#39;s <em>Humanizing Work</em> framework [6]. Each pattern addresses a specific structural reason why a story is too big. The skill auto-detects the best fit, but understanding the patterns transforms you from a tool user into a practitioner.</p>
<p>Think of the eight patterns as a decision tree. You begin by asking: what is the story <em>actually</em> describing? The answer — a user journey, a set of business rules, a data volume problem, and an uncertainty about technology — points you towards the right pattern. The pattern then gives you the cut lines.</p>
<h4>P1: Workflow Steps</h4>
<p>The most common pattern and the right starting point for any user-facing story. If the story describes something a user <em>does</em>, that doing almost always has sequential steps. Each step is a candidate for its own story.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “User can complete checkout” Split: “User can add items to cart” → “User can enter shipping info” → “User can pay and confirm”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The instinct here is correct, and the pattern is forgiving. Even if the steps aren’t perfectly independent technically, they’re independent from a user-value perspective: you can demonstrate each one, get feedback on each one, and ship each one to a subset of users for validation.</p>
<h4>P2: Business Rule Variations</h4>
<p>Business logic is where conditional complexity accumulates silently. A story that says “apply discount logic” is hiding at least three different stories behind a single verb. Each business rule variation — each if condition in the logic — is a candidate for its own story.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “Apply discount logic” Split: “Apply 10% member discount” → “Apply seasonal promo code” → “Apply bulk-order discount”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Splitting by business rule variation does something valuable beyond sizing: it forces acceptance criteria to be precise. You cannot write a good AC for “all discount logic&quot;. You can write one for “member discount applied at checkout&quot;.</p>
<h4>P3: Data Variations</h4>
<p>When a story contains the word “all” — import all customers, migrate all records, support all formats — it is almost always hiding multiple stories. Different data shapes bring different schemas, different validation rules, different edge cases, and often different regulatory requirements.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “Import all customer data” Split: “Import US customer data” → “Import EU customer data” → “Import APAC customer data”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Beyond sizing, this pattern surfaces data-specific risk early. Discovering that EU customer data has GDPR implications <em>after</em> you’ve built a generic import mechanism is expensive. Isolating it into its own story forces the conversation to happen during planning.</p>
<h4>P4: Acceptance Criteria Complexity</h4>
<p>Sometimes a story is correctly scoped in concept, but its acceptance criteria are doing the work of three stories. When a single AC describes multiple independent behaviours — especially ones with additive complexity — each behaviour deserves its own story.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “Search returns results with filters, sorting, and pagination” Split: “Search returns basic results” → “Search with filters and sorting” → “Search with pagination”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>This pattern is particularly useful because it preserves the architectural coherence of the feature while giving the team room to deliver incrementally. Basic search ships first; the team gets real feedback on result quality before investing in filters and pagination.</p>
<h4>P5: Major Effort</h4>
<p>Some stories are large not because they’re conceptually complex, but simply because they involve a lot of repetitive or parallel work. Migrating a legacy system. Refactoring a module. Updating a data model across multiple downstream consumers.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “Rebuild the reporting engine” Split: “Migrate report A to new engine” → “Migrate report B” → “Migrate remaining reports”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Splitting by effort does two things. First, it creates checkpoints: each migrated report is a demo, a validation that the new engine works, and a piece of evidence the approach is sound before committing to the full migration. Second, it makes the work visible in a way that a single 13-point story never does.</p>
<h4>P6: External Dependencies</h4>
<p>External systems — third-party APIs, partner integrations, vendor-provided SDKs — introduce a category of risk that internal architecture doesn’t: you can’t control the timeline, the reliability, or the interface. Isolating the integration into its own story contains that risk.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “Integrate with payment provider” Split: “Set up payment provider sandbox integration” → “Implement live payment flow” → “Handle payment webhooks”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The sandbox story is particularly important. It time-boxes the discovery work — learning the API, understanding the authentication model, identifying edge cases — so that the live implementation story can be estimated with real data rather than assumptions.</p>
<h4>P7: DevOps / Operational Steps</h4>
<p>Operational work is systematically undervalued in backlog management. Deployment pipelines, monitoring dashboards, alerting rules, auto-scaling configuration — these are not finishing touches. They are first-class delivery requirements, and they deserve first-class stories.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: <em>“Launch new service”</em> Split: <em>“Deploy service to staging with basic health checks” → “Add production monitoring and alerting” → “Enable auto-scaling and failover”</em></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Treating operational steps as separate stories has a cultural effect beyond task management. It makes the operational requirements explicit and estimable, and it creates accountability: a story about production monitoring has an owner, an acceptance criterion, and a definition of done.</p>
<h4>P8: Tiny Acts of Discovery</h4>
<p>This is the pattern for unknown unknowns. When a story involves technology the team hasn’t used, an integration whose behaviour is undocumented, or an algorithm whose performance characteristics are untested, the correct response is not a wide-range estimate. It’s a spike.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Original: “Build ML recommendation engine” Split: “Spike: validate recommendation algorithm with sample data” → “Build MVP recommendation feature” → “Optimise and scale recommendation engine”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>A spike is a time-boxed investigation with a specific output: enough information to estimate the subsequent stories. It’s not a story that can “fail” — any outcome that produces that information is a success. Treating uncertainty as a first-class story type is one of the most mature things an agile team can do.</p>
<h3>The Pattern Is in the Story</h3>
<p>The most important principle I’ve taken away from building and using this skill: you don’t reason your way to the right pattern. You read the story and let it tell you.</p>
<p>Picture the eight patterns arranged as a decision tree radiating outward from a central question: what is this story fundamentally about? Stories describing user journeys branch to P1. Stories hiding conditional logic branch to P2. Stories using “all” or “any” branch to P3. Stories with overlapping acceptance criteria branch to P4. Stories that are simply a large branch to P5. Stories touching external systems branch to P6. Stories with operational scope branch to P7. Stories containing uncertainty branch to P8.</p>
<p>Each path through the tree leads to a set of 2–5 candidate sub-stories, each of which is then validated against INVEST before being accepted. The tree is not a rigid algorithm — some stories will exhibit characteristics of multiple patterns, and experienced practitioners will develop a feel for which pattern produces the cleanest slices. But the structure matters. It replaces a vague intuition about “this story is too big” with a systematic interrogation of <em>why</em> it’s too big and <em>where</em> the natural cut lines are.</p>

<h3>What Good Splitting Practice Signals About a Team</h3>
<p>Teams that split well share a common characteristic: they think about delivery from the user backwards, not from the architecture forward. This sounds simple. In practice, it requires resisting a deeply ingrained instinct to decompose by technical boundary — the same instinct that makes engineers good at system design and makes them default to horizontal story splits.</p>
<p>The eight patterns are a translation layer between the way engineers naturally think about systems and the way agile teams need to think about delivery. A practitioner who has internalised them doesn’t need a skill to apply them — they’ve updated the mental model itself. The skill just makes that mental model available to everyone on the team, regardless of experience level, at the moment it’s needed.</p>

<h3>Overview</h3>
<p>We have all been there: a product manager drops a half-sentence feature request in Slack, a production bug needs an immediate ticket, or an engineering spike lacks proper boundaries. The typical result is a backlog filled with ambiguous tickets that dissolve into lengthy debates during sprint grooming.</p>
<p>Instead of fighting the process, we can use the GitHub Copilot extensibility model to build dedicated <strong>Agent Skills</strong> — tailored, prompt-packaged capabilities that run locally or in the cloud.</p>

<h3>Core Concept: What Makes a Story “Good”?</h3>
<p>Before looking at what the AI agent produces, we need to establish what it is optimising for. The agent relies on three core pillars to transform raw text into engineered requirements.</p>
<h4>Framework 1 — INVEST: Your Quality Gate</h4>
<p>Every single output generated by the Agent Skill is validated against the <strong>INVEST</strong> matrix. Coined by Bill Wake in 2003, this six-criterion quality heuristic remains the gold standard for requirement engineering:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Independent:</strong> Can this story be developed and shipped without waiting on another in-progress story?</li>
<li><strong>Negotiable:</strong> Is the implementation approach open, leaving only the outcome fixed?</li>
<li><strong>Valuable:</strong> Does it deliver clear business or user value on its own?</li>
<li><strong>Estimable:</strong> Can the team size it in Fibonacci points with reasonable confidence?</li>
<li><strong>Small:</strong> Can it be comfortably completed within a single sprint?</li>
<li><strong>Testable:</strong> Can the acceptance criteria be objectively verified as pass or fail?</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>What’s happening under the hood:</strong> INVEST isn’t a rubric for post-hoc grading — it is an active gate.</p>
<p><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*8d1rN41k4lvLdnJfK38GBQ.png" alt=""></p>
<p>The Copilot Agent Skill enforces it at <em>Refinement time</em>, not review time. If a story fails the <em>Estimable</em> or <em>Small</em> tests (for example, if it’s too ambiguous or massive), the agent automatically flags the blockages before they can derail sprint planning.</p>
<h4>Framework 2 — BDD Acceptance Criteria: The GIVEN/WHEN/THEN Contract</h4>
<p>The skill defaults to Behavior-Driven Development (BDD) format for acceptance criteria. This isn’t an aesthetic preference; it’s a shared language between product, engineering, and QA that makes criteria completely deterministic.</p>
<p>The GIVEN/WHEN/THEN structure maps directly to verifiable system states:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em><strong>GIVEN</strong></em> <em>the user is on the checkout page</em></p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p><em><strong>AND</strong></em> <em>their cart total exceeds $50</em></p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p><em><strong>WHEN</strong></em> <em>they click “Apply Free Shipping”</em></p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p><em><strong>THEN</strong></em> <em>the shipping cost is set to $0</em></p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p><em><strong>AND</strong></em> <em>a confirmation message appears within 500ms</em></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Compare this to the standard alternative: <em>“Free shipping should apply when eligible.”</em> A developer and a QA engineer reading that phrase in isolation will almost always build and test two completely different implementations.</p>
<p><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*su55ZIrd2rip7xy1V97A8w.png" alt=""></p>
<p><strong>The Key Takeaway:</strong> BDD criteria are executable in your head. Anyone can read them and know exactly what &quot;done&quot; looks like, completely eliminating the interpretation gap at sprint reviews.</p>
<h3>Framework 3 — Scope IN/OUT: The Boundary Fence</h3>
<p>This is the most underrated section in modern project management, and the single most effective tool for preventing mid-sprint scope creep. Every story produced by the skill explicitly outlines its boundaries.</p>
<p>For an engineering or feature task, it looks like this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Scope IN:</strong> Guest checkout, credit card payment, email confirmation.</li>
<li><strong>Scope OUT:</strong> Refund logic, saved payment methods, admin reporting, multi-currency.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The Key Takeaway:</strong> Scope OUT is not a parking lot for future work. It is an explicit contract signed during refinement that protects engineering from well-intentioned, mid-sprint “add-ons.”</p>
<p><img src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*N8nG9UEeWAm5AB-nA0W-uQ.png" alt=""></p>
